The Water Was Never
Yours To Sell.

Data centers need two things above all else — power and water. Rural Colorado has both. The coal transition money is already here. The sealed trust is still sealed. The Gazette is watching the Yampa.
By Rigby Donovan · Ticon Recon Field Office · Craig, Colorado

In 1868, the Ute Reservation covered most of western Colorado and had been granted to the Ute people for their "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" by treaty. The treaty also required the U.S. government to prevent trespass on Ute lands. Within a decade, mining interests had encroached on the reservation with little federal resistance. Governor Pitkin campaigned on "The Utes Must Go." They went. The land opened. The water came with it.

One hundred and forty-seven years later, the same valley's water is being quietly repositioned for the next extraction play. Data centers — the warehouses of the digital economy — consume millions of gallons of water annually for cooling. A single large facility can use more water than a small city. Rural Colorado, with its coal-era power infrastructure, cheap land, cooler temperatures, and federal transition funding, is an increasingly attractive target.

The Craig Gazette has been tracking a sealed trust that reconvened — not publicly, not at the Elks Lodge as announced, but in a private room above a Craig storefront — since Issue 002. Three of seven beneficiary entities have been unsealed. Four remain sealed. The trust has not disclosed its beneficiaries, its assets, or its purpose. Our CORA request targeting the civil cases connected to this trust remains open. The City of Craig has not complied.

The Gazette is not alleging that this specific trust is a data center water acquisition vehicle. We are observing a pattern: sealed instruments, non-disclosed beneficiaries, and water-adjacent legal proceedings in a valley that is being actively targeted by the technology infrastructure industry. That pattern deserves scrutiny. The public record belongs to the public.

The pattern of extraction in this valley is not new. It was coal. Before that, it was cattle and land. Before that, it was the forced removal of the people who lived here for ten thousand years to make room for all of it. Each extraction was framed as progress. Each left the community holding less than it started with. The Gazette is naming the pattern so the community can see it before the next round is decided in a back room.

"Excited Delirium" — What the Diagnosis Means

Gazette Desk · Public Record

What It Is

"Excited delirium" is a controversial diagnosis used by some medical examiners and law enforcement agencies to explain in-custody deaths — typically in cases where physical restraint was used. It describes a state of extreme agitation, hyperthermia, and cardiovascular collapse.

The American Medical Association and the American Psychiatric Association have both raised serious questions about its validity as a cause of death. Critics argue the diagnosis has been disproportionately applied in cases involving Black men and has been used to shield officers from accountability when restraint-related deaths occur.

The Gazette is tracking its use in northwest Colorado. If you have information about a death in Moffat County in which this diagnosis was applied, contact the Gazette. Source identity is protected.

Data Centers Are Coming.
Who Decides Where?

Gazette Desk · Technology & Land

A data center is a warehouse of computers that stores and processes digital information for corporations, governments, and individuals. They run 24 hours a day, generate enormous heat, and require constant cooling — which means constant water consumption and massive electricity draw.

Rural Colorado is being targeted by the data center industry for four reasons: idle coal-era power infrastructure, cheap and abundant land, cooler average temperatures that reduce cooling costs, and federal transition funding that subsidizes "economic development" in coal-impacted communities.

The jobs promised by data centers are largely construction jobs. Once built, a facility the size of several city blocks may employ fewer than two dozen people full-time. The water consumption, however, is permanent and substantial.

The BEANS Alternative

Ticonderoga Systems Holdings is building a distributed edge intelligence network — the opposite of a centralized data center. Instead of sending community data to a corporate warehouse in another state, BEANS processes data locally at each hub node. Craig's data stays in Craig. The community owns the intelligence infrastructure rather than renting access to someone else's.
